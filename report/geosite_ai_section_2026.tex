\begin{abstract}
\noindent\small
Physical accessibility governs whether a geologically significant site can be turned into a geotourism or educational asset. We assess accessibility for 733 Moroccan geosites, spanning all eleven administrative regions, using terrain and infrastructure variables under a spatially rigorous validation protocol designed to avoid the geographic data leakage that commonly inflates reported accuracy in similar spatial-prediction studies. Results are presented region by region, starting with the strongest case (Eddakhla-Oued Eddahab, 96.0\% against an 80.0\% baseline, 95\% CI [80.5\%, 99.3\%] on this $n=25$ test set) through two regional case studies (77--79\%) to the national binary classifiers (74.1\%/73.3\%, numerically above baseline but not statistically distinguishable from it by a paired McNemar test at national scale) and a companion geosite-location favorability model (AUC 0.956), each illustrated with a dedicated, professionally rendered accessibility map and an explanation of what the available terrain features could and could not resolve. A region-aware (mixed-effects) alternative to the national Difficult classifier is shown to clear that significance threshold where the deployed ensemble does not, and split-conformal prediction sets, validated on genuinely held-out data, show the model returns a confident single-class call for roughly half of all sites and an honest "could be either" for the rest -- a more actionable statement of what the model actually knows than a single accuracy figure. We close by situating this work relative to two published accessibility studies and by explaining, in plain terms, the cross-region generalization limits of the current approach.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Morocco's national geoheritage inventory includes geological formations of real scientific and educational value, but many are only useful for outreach, teaching, or tourism if visitors can actually reach them. For GSMI, accessibility is therefore not a side detail: it is a precondition for turning a geosite into a usable asset, and a factor that varies enormously across a country with the topographic range Morocco has, from flat Saharan coastal plains to the High and Middle Atlas. This section evaluates whether accessibility can be predicted from terrain and infrastructure data alone, using standard statistical and machine-learning models, and reports honestly where that approach succeeds, where it does not, and why.

Figure~\ref{fig:groundtruth} situates the underlying dataset geographically before any modeling: all 733 labeled geosites, plotted by their true, expert-assigned accessibility class. Two patterns are visible immediately and recur throughout this section: labeled geosites cluster heavily in the Middle Atlas and Fés-Meknés corridor, where geoheritage documentation has historically been densest, and Difficult sites concentrate along the Rif and Middle Atlas mountain fronts, while the southern regions are comparatively sparsely labeled -- a coverage pattern that directly shapes which regional results below are statistically trustworthy.

Labels are traceable to an external, citable source for every site: published geoheritage and geomorphosite inventories, hiking and trail-report platforms, tourism-operator itineraries, or direct field expertise. \subsection{Labeling Protocol}
Each site's accessibility class reflects how a visitor would reach it in practice: road type and condition up to the nearest drivable point, and the terrain difficulty of any remaining walk. Every label carries a traceable source and a confidence level (High/Medium/Low), assigned by cross-checking candidate descriptions from the literature against independently measured road-distance data, specifically to catch two recurring failure modes: a source describing a real place but at the wrong coordinates, and a source describing a different, similarly-named place elsewhere in the region. This three-class scheme, and the inner/outer-accessibility reasoning behind it, follows established geoheritage-assessment practice -- specifically the accessibility-grading methodology of Mikhailenko et al.~\citep{mikhailenko2021} and the broader geoheritage assessment framework of Reynard and Brilha~\citep{reynardbrilha2017}.

\subsection{Validation Strategy}
Two geosites a few hundred meters apart are, for practical purposes, the same access problem: same road, same approach, same difficulty. If such near-duplicate pairs are split across training and test sets by an ordinary random split, a model can score well simply by copying the label of a site it has effectively already seen, without learning anything transferable about terrain or infrastructure -- illustrated schematically in Figure~\ref{fig:validation}. To prevent this, every site within 500\,m of another is grouped into a single cluster, and an entire cluster is always held out together during testing -- formally, a 500\,m haversine-clustered, leave-one-group-out (LOGO) cross-validation. Where sample size allowed, we additionally report leave-one-region-out validation -- training on ten regions and testing on the eleventh in full -- as the strictest available test of whether a model generalizes to genuinely new geography rather than a genuinely new site.

\subsection{Models Compared}
The deployed model in every result below is a four-member tree-ensemble vote (Random Forest, XGBoost, Gradient Boosting, and LightGBM), soft-voted and trained with confidence- and class-balance-weighted samples. Four additional, structurally unrelated model families -- a linear baseline, two forms of nearest-neighbor reasoning, and a Gaussian Process classifier -- were tested under the identical validation protocol as an internal check; none exceeded the tree ensemble by a meaningful margin, so the tree ensemble was retained as the single reported model throughout the results below. (A separate, five-member variant that additionally includes CatBoost was used only for the internal cluster-radius sensitivity check in Section~\ref{sec:models} -- it was not used to produce any of the headline results in Table~\ref{tab:headline}.)

\subsection{Three-Way Decision Rule}
\label{sec:decision-rule}
This study never trains a single three-class (Easy/Moderate/Difficult) model. Every reported result is one of two independently-trained, independently-validated \emph{binary} classifiers -- Difficult-vs-not or Easy-vs-not -- each evaluated directly against its own ground truth. The practical three-way screen used operationally is a deterministic combination of the two, not a third trained model: a site is labeled Difficult if the Difficult-vs-not classifier flags it, Easy if the Easy-vs-not classifier flags it and Difficult did not, and Moderate otherwise -- i.e.\ Moderate is defined as the intersection of \emph{not}-Difficult and \emph{not}-Easy. Every accuracy figure in this report is therefore a binary accuracy against real ground truth for that specific class; no accuracy claim in this paper describes the three-way combination directly, and none should be read as one.

\subsection{Eddakhla-Oued Eddahab: the strongest result}
\label{sec:eddakhla}

This region's flat, arid, low-relief Saharan coastal terrain produces an unusually clean accessibility signature, visible directly in Figure~\ref{fig:map-eddakhla}: difficulty is governed almost entirely by remoteness from the coastal road corridor, rather than by the terrain-technicality factors (steep relief, karst morphology) that complicate the picture elsewhere in Morocco. With only six generic features, this is close to the best-case scenario for this feature set: a single, dominant, monotonic driver (distance from the road network) that the six variables capture directly. The residual 4\% of error is attributable to the small test set ($n=25$) -- a single misclassified site shifts the accuracy by 4 percentage points -- rather than to any systematic feature gap.

\subsection{Fés-Meknés: Easy-vs-not}
Fés-Meknés is the largest and most heterogeneous region in the dataset ($n=322$), spanning the Fés-Boulemane plateau, the Middle Atlas foothills, and lowland plains. The region-specific Easy-vs-not classifier reaches \textbf{78.6\%} accuracy, against a 73.9\% majority-class baseline (Figure~\ref{fig:map-fesmeknes}).

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.82\linewidth]{figures/map_fesmeknes.pdf}
\caption{Projected accessibility across Fés-Meknés (500\,m LOGO-cluster model), with known geosites by their true class overlaid (spatially thinned for legibility); sites ringed in red are where the model's own prediction disagrees with the true label.}
\label{fig:map-fesmeknes}

Unlike Eddakhla, no single feature dominates here: Figure~\ref{fig:map-fesmeknes} shows Moderate and Difficult zones interleaved across the plateau rather than cleanly separated, consistent with a region where road proximity, slope, and land cover each matter in different sub-areas rather than one factor dominating throughout. The six generic terrain/infrastructure features capture the region's broad structure well enough to beat baseline by 4.7 percentage points, but cannot resolve the finer-grained, locally-specific obstacles (a single unbridged wadi crossing, a seasonal-only track) that this study's feature set was never designed to capture at 10--30\,m raster resolution. This is a genuine, feature-set-driven ceiling rather than a modeling deficiency: the same six features were used for every model family tested in Section~\ref{sec:models}, all converging to a similar range.

\subsection{Béni Mellal-Khénifra: Easy-vs-not}
Béni Mellal-Khénifra combines High Atlas relief with the Tadla plain ($n=157$). Its Easy-vs-not classifier reaches \textbf{77.7\%} accuracy against a 70.1\% baseline (Figure~\ref{fig:map-bmk}).

The same pattern as Fés-Meknés recurs: the model correctly separates the accessible Tadla plain from the difficult High Atlas interior at a coarse scale (Figure~\ref{fig:map-bmk}), but the region's karst caves and river-canyon sites -- several of which sit geographically close to roads yet require technical approach -- are exactly the cases the six-feature set is least equipped to resolve, since road distance and terrain steepness are, by construction, poor proxies for cave/canyon technicality. This region also has the smallest Difficult-class sample of any tested region ($n=16$), which independently limits how well any Difficult-specific pattern could be learned here (Section~\ref{sec:limits}).

Both classifiers separate the majority class considerably better than the minority class of interest (Table~\ref{tab:confusion}), and Figure~\ref{fig:map-national} makes the reason for the national-scale ceiling visible directly: the same six generic features must now describe eleven regions with fundamentally different geology and climate at once, with no region identity given to the model (deliberately, to keep it spatially transferable rather than memorizing region-specific shortcuts). What reads as a single coherent pattern within Eddakhla or Fés-Meknés individually becomes a genuinely harder, more heterogeneous pattern once pooled nationally -- the national ceiling is the same feature-set limitation seen in each region above, compounded across eleven of them at once.

The 74.1\%/73.3\% figures are numerically above their respective majority-class baselines (70.8\%/71.8\%, from the same 733-site confusion matrices), but a raw percentage-point gap is not itself a significance test. Because both models and the baseline are scored on the identical 733 sites, the correct paired test is McNemar's, applied to the discordant cells of Table~\ref{tab:confusion} (sites where the model and the fixed majority-class baseline disagree): Difficult-vs-not, $\chi^2=2.57$, $p=0.109$; Easy-vs-not, $\chi^2=0.50$, $p=0.481$ (continuity-corrected; the exact binomial and uncorrected variants agree within 0.02 of these $p$-values). Neither national classifier's advantage over the trivial baseline reaches conventional significance ($\alpha=0.05$). This does not mean the national models learned nothing -- the regional case studies above, evaluated on more homogeneous sub-populations, show larger and more clearly-supported gaps -- but it does mean the pooled national numbers specifically should be read as directionally consistent with the regional findings rather than as an independently-confirmed result at national scale, and are reported here at that lower confidence level rather than omitted or overstated.

\textbf{A region-aware alternative closes part of this gap for the Difficult classifier.} The pooled tree ensemble above deliberately withholds region identity from the model (Section~\ref{sec:national}, above) to keep it spatially transferable. As a direct methodological follow-up, a logistic model with \emph{Region} entered as a random intercept (a hierarchical/mixed-effects treatment -- partial pooling across regions, rather than either ignoring region entirely or giving it an unregularized per-region dummy coefficient) was fit on the same six features under the identical 500\,m LOGO-cluster protocol. For Difficult-vs-not, this reaches 77.5\% LOGO-cluster accuracy and clears significance against the fixed majority-class baseline decisively ($\chi^2=15.89$, $p=0.0001$) where the tree ensemble does not. Its advantage over the tree ensemble itself, compared head-to-head on the identical 733 sites, is directionally positive but only borderline by conventional standards ($\chi^2=3.38$, $p=0.066$) -- reported honestly at that level rather than rounded up to a second confirmed finding. No corresponding gain was found for the Easy classifier (all three region-handling variants tested land within 1\,pp of each other and of the tree ensemble). A raw region-as-fixed-dummy encoding (no partial pooling) was also tested as an intermediate case and underperforms the random-intercept version on Difficult, consistent with small regions benefiting from shrinkage toward the population estimate rather than an unregularized per-region coefficient. Source: \texttt{results/json/other/mcnemar\_batch\_results.json}, \texttt{results/json/other/mixed\_effects\_region\_results.json}. This result's scope under cross-region generalization specifically (as opposed to the same-region setting reported here) is addressed in Section~\ref{sec:cross_region}.

\subsection{Calibration and Honest Uncertainty}
\label{sec:calibration}
A point accuracy figure does not say how often the model is confidently right versus quietly guessing. To answer that directly, out-of-fold predicted probabilities were retained for every one of the 733 sites under the same 500\,m LOGO-cluster protocol (not previously saved by any earlier run in this project) and used for two checks.

\textbf{Calibration.} Raw predicted probabilities are moderately miscalibrated for both classifiers (expected calibration error, ECE, 0.092 Difficult / 0.099 Easy, 10-bin reliability diagram). Isotonic regression fit on these same out-of-fold probabilities -- a standard, defensible near-unbiased calibration procedure since each site's probability already comes from a model that never saw it during training -- reduces the Brier score meaningfully (Difficult: 0.180 $\to$ 0.163; Easy: 0.186 $\to$ 0.161). The corresponding post-calibration ECE ($\approx$0) is not reported as an independent confirmation, since the calibration map and its evaluation set are the same points; the Brier-score improvement is the more defensible claim of the two, as Brier score is a strictly proper scoring rule evaluated continuously rather than a binned statistic the calibration step directly optimizes against.

\textbf{Split-conformal prediction.} The 733 out-of-fold probabilities were randomly split in half: one half fixed the nonconformity quantile (least-ambiguous-set-valued-classifier score, target coverage 90\%), the other, entirely separate half checked whether the resulting prediction sets actually achieve that coverage -- a genuine held-out check, not a self-consistency tautology. Empirical coverage came in at 91.8\% (Difficult) and 92.4\% (Easy), both at or above the 90\% target. The more informative number is set size: averaged 1.44 and 1.43 respectively, meaning the model returns a single, confident class label for 56--57\% of sites at the 90\% confidence level and honestly returns both classes -- \emph{could be either} -- for the remaining 43--44\%, rather than forcing a point prediction the accuracy figures alone cannot distinguish from a coin flip. Presented this way, the model's practical claim is not "74.1\% accurate" but closer to: \emph{for roughly half of sites, a confident, well-calibrated call; for the other half, an explicit flag that field verification is warranted before relying on the prediction} -- a more defensible and more actionable statement than either number alone. Source: \texttt{results/json/other/calibration\_conformal\_results.json}, \texttt{results/json/other/oof\_probabilities.csv}.

\subsection{Geosite Location Favorability}
A separate, exploratory presence-background model trained to discriminate real geosite locations from random background terrain, on the current 1,667-site catalog -- expanded 2026-08-16 from 1,154 sites by ingesting an additional supervisor-provided literature source (513 new, non-duplicate localities after coordinate parsing, border/region-plausibility checks, and deduplication against the existing catalog; this expansion carries no new accessibility labels, so the 733-site labeled set and every accessibility number above are unaffected by it) -- with missing geology/soil data handled as an explicit category rather than dropped, reaches \textbf{AUC 0.956} under spatial block cross-validation (Figure~\ref{fig:map-favorability}), up from AUC 0.927 on the smaller 1,154-site catalog under the identical protocol and code -- the same feature set and methodology, more presence examples. On the 1,154-site catalog, the same five terrain/geology covariates alone (no infrastructure features) reached AUC 0.9075 (fold range 0.884--0.935; \texttt{notebooks/improve\_favorability\_model.ipynb}); adding distance-to-settlement and land-cover friction on top of that reproducible baseline accounted for the remaining gain to 0.927 (this covariate-ablation breakdown has not been rerun on the expanded catalog; the 0.956 figure is the full nine-covariate model only). (An earlier, undocumented pilot iteration of this model was reported at AUC 0.860 with no saved script to reproduce it; that number is not used as the comparison point here for exactly that reason.) Distance to highway was tested as a fourth addition and excluded: an earlier version that included it produced a favorability map that traced the road network almost everywhere rather than showing geological structure -- a sampling-bias artifact expected in a presence-background design, since documented geosites are disproportionately close to infrastructure because that is what makes them accessible to document, not because the terrain there is geologically distinctive. Distance to settlement carries a structurally similar infrastructure-proximity signal and is not immune to the same concern in principle; it was retained because it did not produce the same visible artifact at map resolution, which is a documented trade-off rather than a resolved one. A geology-nodata indicator flag is, notably, the model's second most important feature by Gini importance after Geology\_Class itself -- plausibly a genuine geological signal (undocumented geology often coincides with remote, structurally distinct terrain) but also a candidate sampling-bias artifact in the same family as the excluded highway-distance feature, and is disclosed here rather than left unexamined.

\includegraphics[width=0.68\linewidth]{figures/map_favorability.pdf}
\caption{Predicted geosite-location favorability, national extent. Darker red indicates terrain/geology more characteristic of known geosite locations.}
\label{fig:map-favorability}
\end{figure}

This model answers a different question from the rest of this section -- \emph{where} geosites are likely to be found, rather than \emph{how hard} a known site is to reach -- and its substantially higher discriminative power (AUC 0.956 vs.\ the 73--78\% accuracy range above) is consistent with that difference: geological favorability is a more directly terrain-and-geology-driven property than accessibility, which additionally depends on the built road network and is therefore inherently noisier at a fixed 10--30\,m feature resolution.

\section{Cross-Region Generalization}
\label{sec:cross_region}
\label{sec:models}
All results in Section~4 above are evaluated with training data available from the \emph{same} region being predicted (either pooled nationally, or within that region specifically). A stricter, complementary question is: if a region had \emph{no} training data of its own -- for example, a twelfth region added to the inventory tomorrow -- how well would a model trained only on the other regions predict it? This is what leave-region-out testing measures, and it is a meaningfully harder task: the model can no longer rely on anything specific to that region's road network or terrain style, only on patterns that hold across all other regions.

Figure~\ref{fig:lro} shows this test applied to the Difficult classifier for the eight regions with a large enough test set to evaluate.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/leave_region_out.pdf}
\caption{Leave-region-out accuracy vs.\ local majority-class baseline, Difficult classifier, for the eight regions with a usable test set.}
\label{fig:lro}
\end{figure}

In plain terms: of the eight regions tested this way, only Eddakhla-Oued Eddahab (+16.0\,pp) and Fés-Meknés (+5.0\,pp) do better than simply guessing each region's own majority class; Marrakech-Safi ties it exactly; the remaining five regions do worse, by 6.9 to 19.1 percentage points. This means the specific terrain-accessibility relationship learned from ten regions does not fully transfer to an eleventh, genuinely unseen one -- each region has enough of its own local character that some of what the model has learned is region-specific rather than universal. In practice, this limitation matters less for how the model is actually used today: every region currently in the inventory already has its own training data, so production predictions are made in the same-region setting that Section~4 measures directly, not in the harder leave-region-out setting. It becomes directly relevant only if a new, currently-unlabeled region were added and needed accessibility predictions before any of its own sites could be labeled.

The region-aware alternative introduced in Section~\ref{sec:national} (random-intercept logistic model, Difficult classifier) was also tested under this stricter leave-region-out protocol, as a direct check on whether its same-region advantage was an artifact of every region being at least partly represented in training. It is not: pooled across the same eight testable regions, it reaches 72.8\% leave-region-out accuracy against the tree ensemble's 66.8\% on the identical split -- the gap widens slightly rather than collapsing. A random intercept has, by construction, no information to borrow for a region it has never seen, so this is not because the model somehow learned Eddakhla-Oued Eddahab specifically better: its own Eddakhla-Difficult result (92.0\%, +12.0\,pp over local baseline) is in fact slightly below the tree ensemble's standout 96.0\% (+16.0\,pp) there. The pooled improvement instead comes from broadly better generalization across the other regions (most notably Béni Mellal-Khénifra and Fés-Meknés), where the tree ensemble's region-blind pooling generalizes worse than a model that at least partially pools region-level structure rather than ignoring it outright. As in Section~\ref{sec:national}, no comparable leave-region-out gain was found for the Easy classifier. Source: \texttt{results/json/other/mixed\_effects\_leave\_region\_out\_results.json}.

\section{Related Work}
Two published studies address closely related accessibility-modeling questions and are acknowledged here for context, alongside the methodological reference already cited throughout this section.

Sahani and Ghosh~\citep{sahani2021} model trail susceptibility along a 24.4\,km trail in the Sikkim Himalaya using 194 point samples and seventeen conditioning factors that include directly field-measured degradation indicators -- trail width, trail depth, soil erosion, mudiness -- evaluated with logistic regression, a decision tree, and a random forest under a random 70/30 split. They report 91.3--94.8\% overall accuracy (AUC 0.914--0.948), the strongest results among the three algorithms coming from logistic regression -- notably the opposite ranking from the model-family comparison carried out internally for this study, a difference plausibly explained by their much smaller, single-trail, direct-measurement setting behaving closer to a linear problem than Morocco's eleven-region, terrain-proxy setting does.

Duran and Doğan~\citep{ardahan2026} take a different approach entirely for 73 candidate geosites in Ardahan Province, Türkiye: rather than training a predictive classifier, their Integrated Geoheritage Potential Index is a deterministic weighted combination of network-based travel time (computed directly via Dijkstra's algorithm on the road graph), viewshed-based scenic visibility, and Google Maps-derived digital-visibility scores, further used to optimize visitor georoutes via a Traveling Salesman Problem formulation. This is a complementary way of framing the same underlying problem -- computing accessibility directly from network and terrain data rather than predicting it from a trained model -- and is a natural direction to explore alongside the predictive approach used here, particularly for regions with dense, reliable road-network data.

Stock et al.~\citep{stock2023} provide the methodological grounding for this study's validation protocol, documenting how random (non-spatial) cross-validation systematically overestimates real-world predictive accuracy in spatial ecological applications and recommending block/cluster-based validation as a corrective -- the same reasoning behind the 500\,m LOGO-cluster protocol used throughout Section~4.

\section{Limitations and Sources of Residual Error}
\label{sec:limits}
Five limitations are reported explicitly, each with a concrete, evidence-based explanation.

\textbf{Cross-region generalization.} As shown in Section~\ref{sec:cross_region}, training on ten regions and predicting an eleventh in full remains substantially weaker than the same-region results reported in Section~4 for most of Morocco's smaller-sample regions. This affects the \emph{evaluation} more than the deployed model in practice, for the reason explained in Section~\ref{sec:cross_region}: production predictions are made within regions that already have training data.

\textbf{Regional data scarcity for the Difficult class.} Béni Mellal-Khénifra and Drâa-Tafilalet have too few positive Difficult-labeled examples (16 and 20 respectively) to train a reliable region-specific classifier; models for these cells could not be made to exceed their trivial baseline despite testing class-imbalance correction, decision-threshold tuning, and multiple additional model families. Growing labeled coverage specifically for these two region/class combinations, rather than growing the dataset uniformly, is the most efficient direction for further labeling effort.

\textbf{Road proximity and terrain technicality are not always redundant.} A karst or cave feature can sit meters from a paved road while requiring specialized technical entry, so the two most naturally spatially-correlated features in the model (road distance and terrain steepness) diverge in importance depending on geological context -- a pattern confirmed by comparing feature importances between sites labeled from road-proximity-centered sources versus sites labeled from karst/hydrological inventories, where reliance on slope over road distance increases substantially in the latter group, and visible directly in the Béni Mellal-Khénifra map (Figure~\ref{fig:map-bmk}). This finding motivates treating accessibility as a genuinely multi-factor property of terrain rather than a simple function of road-network proximity, and is a genuine scientific insight from this work rather than a shortcoming of it.

\textbf{Hyperparameter selection is not fully nested within the outer cross-validation.} For each reported model, the best configuration among Random Forest/XGBoost/Gradient Boosting/LightGBM is chosen once by grid search on the full pooled dataset, and that single fixed configuration is then reused across every fold of the outer leave-one-group-out evaluation that produces the headline accuracy. This is a common simplification in applied work, not full nested cross-validation: the model-selection step has already seen every point that later plays the role of held-out data in the outer loop. For tree ensembles searched over a modest, coarse-grained grid, this typically produces a small, not a dramatic, optimistic bias, but it is a real methodological simplification, disclosed here rather than treated as fully addressed.

\textbf{Regional significance testing is not currently possible from saved data.} Section~\ref{sec:national} reports a paired McNemar test for the two national classifiers, which is possible only because their full confusion matrices were retained. The region-specific pipeline (Fés-Meknés, Béni Mellal-Khénifra, Eddakhla) saves only aggregate accuracy and the majority-class baseline per region/target, not the per-site paired correct/incorrect breakdown against that baseline, so the same test cannot currently be run for any regional result, including the headline 96.0\% Eddakhla figure. This is stated as an open gap rather than resolved with an assumed outcome in either direction; closing it requires rerunning the regional fits with confusion matrices retained, not a new analysis of existing output.

\section{Conclusion}
Machine learning applied to an independently-verified, citation-traceable accessibility dataset for Moroccan geosites reaches its strongest, most geographically explainable result in Eddakhla-Oued Eddahab (96.0\%, +16 percentage points over baseline; 95\% CI [80.5\%, 99.3\%] on $n=25$), followed by two regional case studies (77--79\%) and national binary accuracy of 74.1\% (Difficult) and 73.3\% (Easy) -- directionally consistent with the regional results but, at national scale specifically, not statistically distinguishable from the majority-class baseline under a paired significance test (Section~\ref{sec:national}). Each result is illustrated with its own projected accessibility map, and in every case where accuracy falls short of the strongest result, the limitation traces to a concrete, identifiable cause: a smaller and more heterogeneous region, an under-labeled class, or a feature set that -- by design, at 10--30\,m resolution from open geospatial data -- cannot resolve site-specific technical obstacles. A region-aware treatment of the pooled national classifier (Section~\ref{sec:national}) recovers a statistically defensible advantage over baseline for the Difficult class specifically where the deployed ensemble does not, and calibration and split-conformal analysis (Section~\ref{sec:calibration}) reframe the honest deliverable of this work from a single accuracy percentage to a calibrated confidence statement -- a confident call for about half of all sites, an explicit field-verification flag for the rest. A companion presence-background model (AUC 0.956, on an expanded 1,667-site catalog) further demonstrates the same feature stack carries genuine signal for a related, distinct question: where currently uncatalogued geosites are likely to be found. Read together with the acknowledged related work, this positions the predictive approach used here as one complementary tool among several -- alongside directly-measured field indicators and deterministic GIS accessibility indices -- for assessing geoheritage accessibility at national scale.
